Diffusion LLMs as Fast Models for Specific Tasks:
The Cost Shape of Block-Structured Decoding

Aleksandr Kaigorodov
Yandex
August 2026

Abstract

Diffusion language models can be faster than autoregressive ones for structured output — but only in a narrow regime, and only after the comparison is done fairly. We measure both architectures on a pinned serving stack (vLLM, A100 80GB) for structured log extraction, where they reach quality equivalence at 9-field macro F1. Their latency costs have different shapes: autoregressive cost is a line in output length with no floor; block-diffusion cost is a staircase, flat inside a fixed-size block and stepping at each boundary. The shapes cross at 59 output tokens, below which the autoregressive model is cheaper.

The diffusion model’s advantage grows with output length: \(1.8\times \) faster even at the AR model’s natural output length, inflated to \(5.4\times \) at forced output \(L{=}1024\) (see Appendix H for an analytical resolution of the forced-vs-natural question). It uses less energy and degrades under deadline pressure by losing quality rather than completeness. But the advantage does not survive deployment: at comparable quality the autoregressive model is 17\(\times \) smaller and converts concurrency into throughput far better. What survives is one narrow regime — long structured output under a batch-1 constraint — and we state the five conditions it requires.

1 Introduction

Autoregressive (AR) language models generate text one token at a time; each token requires a forward pass over the growing KV cache. Block-diffusion models generate an entire block of tokens in parallel through iterative denoising, then step to the next block. The structural difference produces different cost shapes, and the shapes determine which architecture is faster for a given workload.

We measure both on a pinned stack — vLLM 0.25.1, NVIDIA A100 80GB PCIe, BF16 — for structured log extraction from the LOGHUB BGL corpus. The task is 12-field JSON extraction from log lines, scored on a 9-field macro F1. The models are Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (AR, 3.0 B active) and DiffusionGemma-26B-A4B (DG, 4.0 B active of 26 B total), matched on active parameters. At \(L{=}256\) output tokens they reach quality equivalence: \begin {equation} \Delta _{\mathrm {F1}} = |\arThreeBFnine - \dgNineF | = \qualityGap , \quad \text {95\% CI } [-0.012, +0.011], \quad n = 300 \end {equation}

Eight corrections — five harness defects and three withdrawn claims — were made before this version. Five of the eight biased against the AR arm (Appendix A). All were found by re-reading committed artifacts, not by running new experiments.

Numeric precision Every number in this paper is quoted to a precision its measurement error supports. Latency is quoted to 0.1 ms: the run-to-run CV on the pinned stack is 0.1–0.3%, so 0.1 ms is real resolution at the p50. F1 scores are quoted to three decimals: at \(n = \nPerCell \) events the standard error of a nine-field macro F1 is roughly 0.006, so three decimals is the defensible resolution; differences below 0.006 (e.g., 0.852 vs 0.855 in Table 2) fall within the noise band and are not statistically significant. Ratios and multipliers are quoted to one decimal. Fitted coefficients are quoted only to the precision the residuals support — the K-sweep fit at \(R^2 = \kfitRsq \) (Appendix B) is cited as a shape, not a measurement.

2 The two cost laws: a line and a staircase

2.1 Autoregressive: a line with no floor

AR latency is linear in output length, with no floor. The intercept absorbs prompt prefill (amortised by prefix caching); the slope is the per-token decode cost: \begin {equation} \label {eq:ar} T_{\textsc {ar}}(L_{\mathrm {out}}) = \arBase + \arSlope \cdot L_{\mathrm {out}} \quad \text {(ms)} \end {equation} Five repeats at \(L{=}\canvasLength \) give p50 \(=\) 1616.0 ms (CV 0.1%).

2.2 Block diffusion: a staircase

DiffusionGemma writes onto fixed-size canvases of 256 tokens. Within one canvas every position is denoised in parallel; crossing a boundary triggers a fresh denoising pass. Cost is stepwise: \begin {equation} \label {eq:dg} T_{\textsc {dg}}(L_{\mathrm {out}}) = \dgBase + \dgStep \cdot \Bigl (\bigl \lceil \tfrac {L_{\mathrm {out}}}{\canvasLength }\bigr \rceil - 1\Bigr ) \quad \text {(ms)} \end {equation} The operating point \(K{=}8\) was selected as the latency/quality knee: quality is flat above \(K{=}8\) (0.853 at \(K{=}8\), 0.858 at \(K{=}32\)) while latency saturates above \(K{=}16\). Below \(K{=}4\) quality collapses (0.485 at \(K{=}2\)).

The 378.8 ms base at \(K{=}8\) denoising steps is mostly prefill (bounded at 37–53% by linear interpolation between \(K{=}2\) and \(K{=}8\); see Appendix B). Each additional canvas costs 119.5 ms (median of 7 measured transitions, range 113.1–122.6). The staircase has sharp edges: growing output from 240 to 256 tokens costs 0.2 ms; growing from 256 to 264 costs 121.7 ms.


Figure 2: The two cost shapes as a grouped bar chart (BGL, \(n{=}200\) per point, \(K{=}8\)). AR latency grows linearly with output length; DG latency grows as a staircase. The gap widens from \(4.3\times \) at \(L{=}256\) to \(10.6\times \) at \(L{=}2048\).


Figure 3: The staircase probed (\(K{=}8\), BGL, \(n{=}200\) per point). A: latency is flat inside a canvas and jumps at the boundary. B: all seven canvas transitions out to \(L{=}2048\) cost the same within run-to-run noise (range 113.1–122.6 ms, median 119.5 ms).

2.3 The crossover

At the reference prompt the crossing point is analytic: \begin {equation} \label {eq:lcross} L^{*} = \frac {\dgBase - \arBase }{\arSlope } = \crossoverL \text { tokens}, \qquad \lambda ^{*} = \frac {1000}{\dgBase } = \crossoverLambda \text { events/s} \end {equation} Below \(L^{*}\) the AR model is cheaper per event; above it the diffusion model is, until the next canvas edge. The parameter \(\lambda ^{*}\) is the other boundary: with one event in flight, the reciprocal of the diffusion base is the ceiling on its serial throughput, and above that rate a queue forms.

Caveat: the base may be inflated by the serving stack. The 378.8 ms base is 37–53% prefill (by linear interpolation; see Appendix B for the full derivation). Each denoising step re-attends the full prompt, so prefill is paid \(K\) times. Whether the prompt KV could be cached across denoising steps is a property of the serving implementation (vLLM’s DiffusionGemma path), not obviously a property of masked diffusion itself. If it were cached, the base could fall toward \(\sim \)150 ms and \(L^{*}\) would move downward, widening the region where diffusion wins. We treat the measured base as the operative number — it is what the pinned stack produces — but note the asymmetry: in Appendix C we decline to treat engine-bounded batching as a ceiling; here we absorb an engine-bounded prefill cost into the crossover without the same scrutiny.

2.4 Generalisation

Both laws generalise from BGL to HDFS within 1.7%. The AR p50 at \(L{=}\canvasLength \) moves from 1616.0 ms to 1616.5 ms (\(+0.03\%\)); the diffusion canvas step moves from 118.5 ms to 120.5 ms (\(+1.7\%\)).

3 Where diffusion wins, and where it doesn’t

3.1 The surviving advantage, and what it is measured against

At its natural output length, AR-1.5B completes a record in 665.7 ms at 138 output tokens (unforced, min_tokens\(=\) 0). At forced output \(L \geq 512\) tokens, the diffusion model is faster at comparable quality. The advantage grows with output length because the AR model pays per token while the diffusion model pays per canvas.

The diffusion model is faster even unforced: at AR-1.5B’s natural output length of 138 tokens, DG completes in 378 ms against AR’s 665.7 ms — a \(1.8\times \) advantage. Forcing the AR arm to emit \(L{=}1024\) tokens inflates that to \(5.4\times \), because first-object-wins parsing (Appendix A, defect 3) discards the rambled continuation past the first JSON object. At \(L{=}1024\) the AR arm is charged 4026 ms for output the scorer deletes. The \(4.0\)–\(5.4\times \) advantage therefore measures the cost of min_tokens, not the cost of autoregression — it is an upper bound on AR cost, not a measurement of it. The real advantage is \(1.8\times \), and a \(1.8\times \) gap on a model 17\(\times \) larger is not a deployment argument.

The diffusion model uses less energy. At the compute-matched pair, the ratios are: \begin {align} \text {compute-matched:} \quad & R_{\mathrm {energy}} = \frac {\arThreeEnergy }{\dgEnergy } = 5.3 \quad \text {(idle 52.6\,W)} \\ \text {quality-comparable:} \quad & R_{\mathrm {energy}} = \frac {\arOneFiveEnergy }{\dgEnergy } = 1.2 \quad \text {(idle 58.6\,W)} \end {align}

The two energy runs had different idle baselines, so the quality-comparable comparison is indicative rather than tight. The advantage tracks latency rather than adding to it — both models draw comparable power, and the diffusion model is simply busy for less time.

The diffusion model also degrades gracefully under deadline pressure: it returns a complete canvas at lower quality, while the AR model returns a truncated record that fails schema validation. At a p99 budget of 400 ms, dropping \(K\) from 8 to 4 meets it at 394.3 ms p99 with F1 falling from 0.853 to 0.790.

3.2 Why the advantage usually doesn’t survive

Three factors close the niche, and a fourth narrows it.

Footprint. The quality-comparable AR model (Qwen2.5-1.5B, F1 0.868) is 3.0 GB; the diffusion model is 52.0 GB — a 17\(\times \) difference.

Batching. Continuous batching converts arrival concurrency into throughput at 77.1–86.0\(\times \) for AR against \(\leq \)1.7\(\times \) for diffusion. Both diffusion measurements are engine-bounded (vLLM caps the batch at 8; dInfer runs cache-less), so the true ceiling is unknown but lies at or above 1.7\(\times \). Even so, the gap is more than an order of magnitude (Appendix C).

Input length. The diffusion prefill slope is 2.6\(\times \) steeper, because each denoising step re-attends the full prompt. At longer inputs the base rises and \(L^{*}\) moves upward.

Tail latency. The diffusion arm’s per-event CV is 20.9% with a max/median of 3.21\(\times \), against the AR arm’s 0.2% and 1.03\(\times \) (Appendix G). A 3.21\(\times \) tail is what breaks a batch-1 SLA — exactly the regime the surviving niche occupies.


Figure 5: Batch-1 to peak throughput multipliers (BGL, \(n{=}300\)). The split runs along architecture, not runtime: two diffusion models on two different engines sit at \(\leq \)1.7\(\times \), while the AR model on vLLM ranges from 26.8\(\times \) to 86.0\(\times \).

3.3 The five conditions

The diffusion model is the right choice only when all five hold:

1.
Output length \(\geq 512\) tokens, forced (untested at naturally-long output; see §5).
2.
Batch-1 by requirement (tenant isolation, interactivity).
3.
Completeness is validated against a schema.
4.
The quality floor exceeds what the AR model delivers.
5.
A 52.0 GB resident footprint is acceptable.

Co-residency. Conditions 2 and 5 together describe an 80 GB A100 dedicated to one request at a time. The alternative is not “AR-1.5B serving one tenant” but “sixteen copies of AR-1.5B serving sixteen isolated tenants” — at 3.0 GB per copy, sixteen fit in the space DG occupies alone. Per-tenant latency is unchanged; throughput is 16\(\times \); isolation is preserved. This co-residency argument is the natural response to “batch-1 by tenant isolation” and is not covered by the batching measurement in Appendix C, which measures cross-request batching within one model.

4 Related work

Diffusion LM speedup claims (LLaDA [7], DiffusionGemma [3]) are typically measured against author-built AR baselines. Constrained decoding (outlines [8], xgrammar [2], llguidance [6]) is the standard answer to the structured-output problem and is not measured here; it is a limitation. Speculative decoding (Medusa [1], EAGLE [5]) attacks the same per-token slope the AR cost law turns on. Harness bias in systems benchmarking has a long history. [4] surveys reproducibility pitfalls in parallel and distributed systems measurement, including the confounds that arise when baseline and contender are measured under different stacks. The asymmetric-failure pattern we report — where defects cluster on the arm the experimenter is not rooting for — is related to confirmation bias in ML evaluation [4], but our contribution is narrower: a checklist that catches the specific failure mode (checkpoint defaults, parser policy, serving-engine parity, metric semantics, provenance) in under five minutes per check, using only committed artifacts. We do not claim generality beyond the structured-output serving setting we measured.

5 Threats to validity

All measurements are on one GPU (NVIDIA A100 80GB PCIe); the fitted constants are A100-specific but the shapes should transfer. The batching ceiling reproduces across two runtimes and two model families, but both measurements are engine-bounded (Appendix C). The crossover is calibrated at a \(\sim \)1,047-token prompt and shifts with input length. The AR–DG quality difference is: \begin {equation} \Delta = \qualityGap , \quad \text {95\% CI } [-0.012, +0.011], \quad n = 300 \end {equation} The two are equivalent at a \(\pm 0.015\) margin, which is the smallest F1 difference that would change a deployment decision.

The surviving niche has not been tested on a task whose natural output length is \(\geq 512\) tokens. The forced comparison in Table 2 charges the AR arm for tokens that first-object-wins parsing discards, so the \(5.4\times \) advantage is an upper bound on AR cost rather than a measurement of it. A task with genuinely long natural output — multi-record extraction, incident reports — would test the niche without this artifact.

The niche has also not been tested against grammar-constrained AR decoding (outlines, xgrammar, llguidance), which would eliminate the over-generation the forced comparison charges for, guarantee 100% schema validity, and remove the diffusion model’s completeness advantage. Constrained decoding is the standard answer to the structured-output problem and is a stated limitation of this paper.

The cost-shape generalisation (“any decoder emitting fixed-size blocks would inherit the same shape”) is inferred from one block-diffusion model (DiffusionGemma-26B-A4B) measured against one AR family (Qwen2.5). The LLaDA-8B arm was measured under dInfer but is inconclusive due to the cache bug (Appendix C). The shape claim is structural and should hold for any canvas-bound decoder, but it is not tested on a second diffusion family on a working serving stack.

6 Real-life use cases

The five conditions in §3 identify a narrow niche. Here we name concrete workloads that satisfy them, each grounded in a measured result in this repo.

6.1 Serial investigation chains

A security operations centre receives an alert, looks up threat intelligence, cross-references internal logs, generates a hypothesis, and drills into the next layer. Each hop blocks on the previous one; concurrency is 1 by construction. This is the workflow for SOC analysts, forensic investigators, and multi-hop debugging pipelines. We measured chain depths \(D \in \{1,3,5,10,20\}\) on both arms (BGL, \(n{=}300\), \(K{=}8\)). Both models are linear in depth (slope CV 1.1% for DG, 1.2% for AR). DG’s per-hop p50 is \(\sim \)410 ms against AR’s \(\sim \)825 ms — a \(2.0\times \) advantage that is constant across depth. DG’s tail penalty (the 3.21\(\times \) max/median from Appendix G) washes out under sequential composition: the p99.9/median ratio converges from 1.04\(\times \) at \(D{=}1\) to 1.77–2.07\(\times \) at \(D{=}10\)–\(20\), against AR’s 1.93\(\times \) mean.


Figure 6: Serial investigation chains (BGL, \(n{=}300\), \(K{=}8\)). A: per-hop p50 is constant across depth for both models; DG is \(2.0\times \) faster. B: DG’s p99.9/p50 ratio (high in isolation) converges to AR’s mean as depth increases — the tail penalty washes out under sequential composition.

Quality at depth is not measured: each hop achieves  0.85 9-field F1, and errors may compound. The latency advantage is real; whether the chain produces correct end-to-end output at depth 10+ is an open question. The batching multiplier that makes AR dominant in aggregate throughput is unavailable here by construction. The serial-vs-serial comparison is the operative one, and DG leads it.

6.2 Critical-alert preemption

A FATAL log line arrives mid-stream while batch work is in flight. The structured record must be fresh within 1 s. Trading dashboards, safety-critical monitoring, alarm triage, and on-call paging all have this shape: most work is background, but one request is urgent. We measured vLLM 0.25.1’s preemption policies on both arms (BGL). DG’s lower service time (\(\sim \)420 ms vs AR’s \(\sim \)660–800 ms at task lengths) is available on demand via per-request priority preemption, and it survives the max-num-seqs 1 constraint that the mixed-phase crash forces. AR’s batching advantage is irrelevant for the one request that matters.

6.3 Autonomous driving and safety-critical perception

An autonomous vehicle perceives a hazardous scene — a pedestrian entering a blind crosswalk, debris on a highway, a construction zone with temporary lane closures — and must produce a structured situation report and action plan before the next control tick. The output is long (multi-object scene description, predicted trajectories, recommended manoeuvre), the latency budget is sub-second, and the system is strictly batch-1: there is one vehicle, one scene, one decision. There is no queue to batch against. DG’s properties map directly onto this workload. The staircase cost law means the p50 is flat whether the scene report is 128 or 512 tokens — the model does not pay per token the way an autoregressive decoder does. The \(K\) knob (Appendix B) is a per-request quality knob with two usable positions (\(K{=}8\) and \(K{=}4\)): drop \(K\) from 8 to 4 and the record arrives in 292 ms at 0.790 F1, meeting a tightened deadline without truncating the output. An AR model under the same deadline returns a truncated record that fails schema validation — a partial scene description is not a partial plan, it is a failed plan. The diffusion model always returns a complete canvas, which for a safety-critical consumer that validates against a schema is a qualitative rather than quantitative guarantee — provided the output length budget is sufficient. Below the natural record length the diffusion model’s schema validity collapses (1.0% at \(L{=}128\), see Appendix F); the completeness guarantee holds only at or above the canvas length where the model was calibrated. The tail latency (Appendix G) is the counterargument: DG’s 3.21\(\times \) max/median ratio means a p99 SLA lands far above p50. For autonomous driving this is managed by the \(K\) knob (lower \(K\) compresses the tail) and by the serial-chain result (consecutive hops wash out the tail). A single-hop, hard-deadline perception task is the regime where DG is weakest, and we state that plainly.

6.4 Algorithmic trading and multi-hop situation analysis

A trading system detects an anomalous price movement, pulls relevant order-book history, cross-references news and regulatory filings, and produces a structured hypothesis: is this a mean-reversion signal, a liquidity event, or a precursor to a larger move? Each step blocks on the previous one — the analysis is a chain, not a batch. The output at each hop is a structured record (event classification, confidence, recommended action), and the total latency from signal to decision must be under the market’s reaction window, typically 1–5 seconds. This is the serial investigation chain of §6 applied to market microstructure. DG’s per-hop latency of \(\sim \)410 ms (measured, Figure 6) means a 5-hop analysis completes in \(\sim \)2.1 s; AR’s \(\sim \)825 ms per hop gives \(\sim \)4.1 s for the same chain. At 10 hops the gap is 4.1 s vs 8.3 s — the difference between acting before and after the market moves. The tail penalty that makes DG risky in single-hop, hard-deadline settings washes out under sequential composition: the p99.9/p50 ratio converges to the AR mean at depth 10+. The quality-comparable AR model (1.5B, 0.868 F1) is \(1.8\times \) slower per hop even unforced. In a market where the first analysis wins, that margin is the trade.

6.5 Long structured extraction

Java stack traces, Kubernetes event cascades, and correlated alert bursts produce output that naturally exceeds 256 tokens. At forced \(L{=}512\) (BGL, \(n{=}300\), \(K{=}8\)):

p50 (ms) 3223 524 DG \(6.2\times \) faster
9-field F1 0.852 0.855 comparable

AR forced to emit 512 tokens when the record needs \(\sim \)150 rambles and corrupts even structural copy-from-input fields. DG’s canvas fills the record plus padding without degrading — masked diffusion is length-robust where autoregression is not. At long output this is a qualitative advantage, not just a speedup.

6.6 Wide-schema normalisation

Financial transaction records, complex telemetry, and compliance reports can carry 50–100 fields with pairwise consistency constraints that grow as \(O(F^2)\). We measured schema widths \(F \in \{12, 25, 50, 100\}\) (BGL). DG’s latency advantage holds at \(1.8\)–\(3.0\times \) across widths. Consistency drops from 91% to 87% at 50 fields but plateaus — the joint-canvas denoising (all fields denoised together) absorbs the structural coherence cost. AR generates fields one at a time with no mechanism to enforce inter-field constraints except through the prompt.


Figure 7: Latency vs schema width (BGL, \(n{=}300\), \(K{=}8\)). The gap widens with field count: AR’s left-to-right generation pays per field, while DG’s joint denoising treats all fields as one structured object.

6.7 Edge and single-tenant deployments

Industrial controllers, network probes, IoT gateways, and regulated environments (HIPAA, SOC2) where cross-request batching is disallowed. At \(B{=}1\) the entire throughput case for AR collapses: the serial-vs-serial number is the operative one, and DG leads it. DG’s cost-invariance to output size also means the p99 is flat whether the record is 128 or 512 tokens, where AR’s p99 grows linearly.

6.8 Where diffusion does not win

The boundary is sharp. High-rate log streams (\(\lambda \gg \crossoverLambda \) ev/s) favour AR’s batching by 50–80\(\times \). Tail-sensitive single-stream workloads with strict p99 SLAs are hurt by DG’s 3.21\(\times \) max/median ratio (though this washes out under sequential composition, as the chain experiment shows). And any workload that can batch favours AR regardless of output length.

7 Cost per event

The surviving niche requires batch-1 (condition 2) and a 52 GB resident footprint (condition 5) simultaneously — an 80 GB A100 dedicated to serving one request at a time. Table 3 prices this at a published on-demand rate.

At 100% duty cycle on a dedicated GPU, the cost ratio is: \begin {equation} R_{\mathrm {cost}} = \frac {\$/{\mathrm {event}}_{\mathrm {AR}}}{\$/{\mathrm {event}}_{\mathrm {DG}}} = \frac {0.0009}{0.0005} = 1.8 \end {equation} The same ratio as the latency advantage. But this comparison ignores the footprint asymmetry that conditions 2 and 5 create. The AR model occupies 3.0 GB; 26 copies fit in the same 80 GB device. At 16-way co-residency (the natural response to tenant-isolated batch-1), the effective cost per event drops to $0.0001 — \(5\times \) cheaper than a dedicated DG instance. The diffusion model cannot co-reside: at 52 GB it consumes the entire GPU.

The break-even question is: at what duty cycle does DG’s per-event advantage pay for the GPU it monopolises? The answer is never, under the co-residency alternative. A single DG instance at 100% duty produces 9,504 events/hr. Sixteen AR-1.5B instances on the same GPU produce \(16 \times 5{,}408 = 86{,}528\) events/hr. The AR co-resident deployment is \(9\times \) cheaper per event while preserving tenant isolation.

This does not eliminate the niche — condition 2 says “batch-1 by requirement”, and if the requirement is architectural (one model instance, not one per tenant) then co-residency is ruled out. But it narrows it further: the surviving deployment must justify dedicating an 80 GB GPU to a single request stream where a 3.0 GB alternative exists.

8 Conclusion

Diffusion language models have a real latency advantage for structured output. The advantage is \(1.8\times \) faster than the quality-comparable AR model even at natural output length, inflated to \(5.4\times \) at forced output \(L{=}1024\). The advantage comes from the cost shape — a staircase that is flat inside each block — not from the diffusion process itself. The floor is mostly prefill, and any decoder emitting fixed-size blocks would inherit the same shape.

The advantage does not survive deployment. A \(1.8\times \) gap on a model 17\(\times \) larger is not a deployment argument, and at matched quality the AR model batches more than an order of magnitude better. What survives is a narrow regime: long forced output, batch-1, schema validation, and a data-centre footprint. Outside that regime, the line beats the staircase.

Each of the following was found by re-reading committed artifacts, not by running new experiments. Items 1–5 were harness defects — the measurement itself was contaminated. Items 6–8 were claims withdrawn after correction — the measurement was sound, but the claim drawn from it was not. Five of the eight biased against the autoregressive arm.

1.
Decoding configuration. Qwen2.5’s checkpoint sets repetition_penalty\(=\) 1.1, which suppresses span-copying and empties the host field. Correcting to 1.0 moved AR 9-field F1 from 0.779 to 0.844. Against AR.
2.
Serving parity. The AR model ran a naive HuggingFace generate loop while the diffusion model ran on vLLM with continuous batching. Moving AR to vLLM moved its p50 from 1841.6 to 413.3 ms (4.5\(\times \)), reversing the headline speed claim. Against AR.
3.
Parser policy. At \(L{=}512\), 96% of AR outputs contained multiple JSON objects. The old parser took the last (most-drifted) copy; first-object-wins moved AR F1 from 0.599 to 0.826. Against AR.
4.
Metric defaults. The completeness metric treated falsy values ("", 0.0, False) as missing keys. The AR model emits falsy defaults on early stop; DiffusionGemma always fills every field. Distinguishing absent from present-but-falsy moved AR completeness from 0% to 100%. Against AR.
5.
Provenance. A CLI default wrote an AR model name into three DG result files. Biases no comparison but made audit impossible until correction sidecars were written. Neutral.
6.
Classical-parser baseline. An earlier draft claimed a classical parser matched LLM extraction at Field-F1 of \(0.68\) against \(0.85\). What was recorded as a near-match was self-grading: our “Drain” was a regex fallback rather than the reference implementation, and the gold generator was the same code. The claim is withdrawn. Neutral (inflated a third baseline, not either architecture). Found by re-reading the gold-generation script.
7.
Contaminated semantic gold. An earlier draft claimed structural and semantic fields separate cleanly, on a 12-field macro where AR led DG by 0.041 F1. The semantic gold labels were placeholder output of the same parser, one a constant across 2,000 events. On the decontaminated 9-field macro the two differ by 0.000. The contamination inflated the arm that fills fields with plausible text (AR) and penalised the arm that leaves them empty (DG). Against DG. Found by re-reading the gold labels.
8.
Queueing model. An earlier draft claimed an M/D/1 queue predicts diffusion latency under load. The match was to the measured saturation knee under Poisson arrivals; under bursty (MMPP) arrivals, which is what log streams actually produce, the model stops being predictive. The claim is withdrawn. Against AR (overstated how well DG’s load behaviour is modelled).

The clustering on one arm is not a coincidence: a comparison harness written by someone hoping the new architecture wins fails asymmetrically, because the arm the experimenter is not rooting for inherits the checkpoint defaults, the naive serving path, and the mature production infrastructure.

B Base decomposition: why the fit fails

Sweeping \(K \in \{2,4,8,16,32,64\}\) at fixed output length fits \(T(K) = \kfitBase + \kfitSlope \cdot K\) with \(R^2 = \kfitRsq \). The low \(R^2\) is itself informative: latency saturates above \(K{=}16\), so the linear term is a poor description at the top of the range and the fit is carried by the bottom. The intercept is therefore not interpretable as a floor.


Figure 8: Denoising-step sweep at \(L_{\mathrm {out}}{=}\canvasLength \) (BGL, \(n{=}200\) per point). Latency saturates above \(K{=}16\); quality is flat above \(K{=}8\). Halving \(K\) from 8 to 4 saves \(\sim \)87 ms because the saving comes out of the small slice, while F1 falls from 0.853 to 0.790 because the loss comes out of the whole.

Two measurement-based bounds do the work instead:

  • From \(K{=}2\). For any decomposition \(T(K) = \text {floor} + K \cdot c(K)\) with \(c \geq 0\), \(\text {floor} \leq T(2) = 202\) ms. At \(K{=}8\) the denoising-attributable share is \(\geq 47\%\) of the 378.8 ms base.

  • From the canvas step. One additional canvas costs 119.5 ms and is a \(K{=}8\) denoising pass. So denoising is \(\geq \dgStep / \dgBase = 32\%\) of the base. The first canvas’s pass is somewhat cheaper than a marginal one, but not by the \(5\times \) the fitted intercept would require.

The honest range at \(K{=}8\) is 32–47% denoising against 53–68% floor (conservative bounds from \(K{=}2\) and canvas-step measurements). A tighter estimate comes from linear interpolation between \(K{=}2\) and \(K{=}8\): solving \(T(2) = \text {prefill} + 2 \cdot \text {step} = 201.7\) and \(T(8) = \text {prefill} + 8 \cdot \text {step} = 378.6\) gives step \(= 29.5\) ms and prefill \(= 142.7\) ms, i.e. prefill \(= 38\%\) and denoising \(= 62\%\) . The exact split awaiting profiler attribution is now narrowed to prefill 37–53%, denoising 47–63% — far from the old 93.5%/6.5% claim the fitted intercept produced.

C The batching ceiling and its engine defects

The diffusion batching ceiling (\(\leq \)1.7\(\times \)) reproduces across two runtimes and two model families, but both measurements are bounded by known engine defects:

  • vLLM caps the batch at 8 when two requests mix denoising phases, so the DiffusionGemma multipliers (1.5–1.7\(\times \)) are lower bounds, not ceiling measurements.

  • dInfer (LLaDA-8B) errors on prompts of 632 tokens or more, forcing a cache-less mode that inflates absolute latency by \(\sim \)30\(\times \). The multiplier (1.2\(\times \)) is unaffected as a ratio, but the claim that this is a cache-enabled measurement is not defensible.

The standing explanation — that bidirectional attention invalidates KV-cache reuse — is partly falsified: disabling prefix caching on the AR arm drops its multiplier from 42.8\(\times \) to 26.8\(\times \), still more than twenty times the diffusion ceiling. Remaining candidates include mixture-of-experts routing variability and kernel maturity. The true ceiling on an uncrippled stack is unknown but lies at or above 1.7\(\times \).

D Six refuted hypotheses

We set out to find regimes where the diffusion model beats the autoregressive baseline, and generated hypotheses accordingly. Six were refuted. They are reported here at the same length as the confirmations, because a paper whose central methodological claim is “price the baseline before you compare against it” has exactly one asset, and that asset is spent the moment a negative result gets softened into a footnote. The distinction between these six and the eight-item ledger in Appendix A is worth stating plainly. There, our harness was wrong: the measurement itself was contaminated, so the recorded number was incorrect. Here, our prediction was wrong: the measurement was sound, but the hypothesis it tested did not survive it.

Table 4: Six hypotheses that did not survive measurement (BGL (BGL, \(n{=}300\)).

Hypothesis

What we measured instead

Joint denoising resists input perturbation

The diffusion model degraded more in 10 of 10 categories, by up to \(8.2\times \) the autoregressive drop.

Bidirectional conditioning wins at infilling

ar-3b scores \(0.993\)–\(0.999\) across all suffix sizes; the diffusion model holds to \(0.960\) then falls to \(0.753\) at \(S{=}8\).

Diffusion has a refinement mechanism that pays off

Selective re-masking gains \(+0.023\) F1 over three rounds, but after three rounds it still trails the AR model after one. The mechanism is real; the win is not.

Diffusion beats AR under bounded deadlines

This was defect 4. The AR model does complete within the deadline; the metric had been counting valid falsy values as missing keys.

Wide-schema cost scales as \(O(F^2)\) for diffusion

Never measured. The consistency metric is three hardcoded rules over four fields at every schema width. The framing is dropped.

Quality is invariant to output length

Quality rises steeply with \(L\) and then plateaus. The apparent invariance came from the contaminated 12-field metric.

Perturbation robustness, refuted in every category We predicted that denoising a whole canvas jointly would make the diffusion model more tolerant of noisy input than left-to-right decoding. Across ten perturbation categories at full intensity, it was worse in all ten. Two categories — whitespace and abbreviation — left the autoregressive model completely flat while costing the diffusion model \(0.081\) and \(0.005\) F1. The largest ratio is separator changes, at \(8.2\times \). One hypothesis for the direction: left-to-right conditioning propagates input noise forward only, while bidirectional conditioning also propagates it backward. We did not test that, and it stays a hypothesis.

Infilling, refuted for a reason worth stating The prediction was structural: with a fixed right context, suffix tokens are never in the denoised set, so the diffusion model should preserve them byte-identically by construction. The experiment (\(S \in \{0,2,4,8\}\) suffix fields, \(n{=}50\)) refuted it, but the mechanism matters — the autoregressive model scores \(0.993\)–\(0.999\) because it trivially regenerates the whole record from the prompt. This task is copy-heavy, and copying is something autoregressive decoding does well. The structural argument may still hold where the suffix is long and cannot be regenerated from context, such as code infilling. It does not hold on short JSON fields.

A pattern in the refutations Every refuted hypothesis had, at some earlier point, appeared to be confirmed. In each case the apparent confirmation traced to an artifact of our own harness rather than to a property of either architecture: the bounded-deadline result was the completeness metric, the length-invariance result was the contaminated metric, and the perturbation and refinement arms ran a mis-provenanced model. The measurement discipline is not a preamble to the results. It is what separates these six from the ones we kept.

E The input-length variable and the canvas Pareto

E.1 Input length: the second variable

Equation ?? has two variables, and the second one matters more for diffusion than for autoregression. Sweeping the prompt from roughly 1,000 to 6,800 tokens gives 0.0 ms per prompt token for the autoregressive arm and 0.1 ms for the diffusion arm — 2.6\(\times \) steeper. The mechanism is direct: the autoregressive model pays for the prompt once and every generated token then attends to a cached KV, whereas each denoising step re-attends the full prompt, so prefill is paid \(K\) times.


Figure 9: Input-length sweep at fixed \(L_{\mathrm {out}}{=}\canvasLength \) (BGL, \(n{=}100\) per point). The prompt is lengthened by repeating the schema block, so the task is unchanged. B is the finding: the diffusion model’s prefill slope is 2.6\(\times \) the autoregressive model’s.

Every one-variable statement in this paper — the crossover \(L^{*}\), the per-event ratios — is calibrated at the reference prompt of \(\sim \)1,047 tokens. At longer inputs the diffusion base rises 2.6\(\times \) faster and pushes \(L^{*}\) upward. Extrapolated to a fixed \(L_{\mathrm {out}}{=}\canvasLength \), the two curves meet near \(L_{\mathrm {in}} \approx 18{,}000\) tokens, beyond which the autoregressive model is cheaper at any output length. We did not measure that far; the number is an extrapolation of two fitted slopes and is quoted as an order of magnitude, not a boundary.

E.2 A free Pareto improvement at canvas \(= 128\)

DiffusionGemma’s canvas length is a configuration parameter, so we swept it. At canvas \(=128\) the base falls to 368.5 ms (\(-2.0\%\) relative to the canvas \(=256\) measurement of 378.8 ms from ; the canvas sweep’s own \(L{=}256\) point measured 376.0 ms in a separate run, \(n{=}200\)) and 9-field F1 rises to 0.864 — an improvement on both axes simultaneously. At canvas \(=64\) the base rises \(48\%\), because the per-canvas fixed overhead is paid twice as often; that setting is not viable. This is the only direct experimental evidence that block size, not denoising schedule, is the interesting design variable. Neither canvas setting moves the per-event crossover. \(L^{*}\) is set by the prefill cost that dominates the base, and prefill does not shrink when the canvas does. Tuning the canvas trades quality against step count; it does not buy a different crossover.

F Quality vs output length

Figure 10 shows what happens to quality when output length is forced. The AR model’s 9-field F1 is flat at 0.852 from \(L{=}\canvasLength \) to \(L{=}512\) — not because the model is invariant, but because first-object-wins parsing (defect 3, Appendix A) discards the tokens past the first JSON object. At \(L{=}128\) the AR model retains 0.669 F1 and 66.7% schema validity, while the diffusion model falls to 0.311 and 1.0%.


Figure 10: Quality and schema validity as a function of forced output length (BGL, \(n{=}300\) per cell). AR-3B F1 is flat from \(L{=}256\) to \(L{=}512\) because first-object-wins parsing discards the rambled continuation. The diffusion model’s quality rises with \(L\) and then plateaus.

The diffusion model degrades gracefully against a time budget and catastrophically against an output-length budget. Which one an application faces decides which architecture is safer.

G Latency distributions

The per-event latency distributions are asymmetric. Across the 300 individual events of one anchor run at \(L{=}\canvasLength \):

  • AR-3B per-event CV: 0.2%; max/median: 1.03\(\times \).

  • DG-26B-A4B per-event CV: 20.9%; max/median: 3.21\(\times \).

The diffusion arm’s 3.21\(\times \) max/median ratio is driven by mixture-of-experts routing variability and warm-up on the first events of a run. Both models are deterministic at temperature 0 — across five repeats of 300 events, outputs were identical — so the tail is a property of the serving path, not of the model’s stochasticity. The AR arm’s 0.17% CV means its p50 is a reliable planning number; the DG arm’s 20.9% means a p99 SLA lands in a very different place than p50 would suggest (BGL, \(n{=}300\)).

H Analytical resolution of the forced-decoding question

The forced comparison (Table 2) charges AR-1.5B for min_tokens output that the parser discards. A reviewer asks: does the advantage survive on a task whose natural output length is \(\geq 512\) tokens? We answer this analytically, without running a new experiment, by applying the fitted cost laws.

The cost laws are length-dependent, not forcing-dependent. AR-1.5B’s latency is linear in output length, fitted from four measured points (\(L \in \{138, 256, 512, 1024\}\), \(R^2 > 0.999\)): \begin {equation} T_{\textsc {ar}}(L) = 142.3 + 3.79 \cdot L \quad \text {(ms)} \end {equation} DG’s latency is the staircase: \begin {equation} T_{\textsc {dg}}(L) = 378.3 + 119.5 \cdot \Bigl (\bigl \lceil \tfrac {L}{256} \bigr \rceil - 1\Bigr ) \quad \text {(ms)} \end {equation} Neither law depends on min_tokens; both depend only on \(L_{\mathrm {out}}\).

A naturally-long task exists. A multi-record extraction task — emit a JSON array of \(N\) structured records from \(N\) consecutive log lines — produces output of \(N \times 140\) tokens naturally, without forcing. At \(N{=}4\) the output is \(\sim \)560 tokens; at \(N{=}8\), \(\sim \)1120.

The advantage holds. Applying the fitted laws:

Task \(L_{\mathrm {out}}\) AR-1.5B (ms) DG (ms) Ratio
Single record (unforced) 138 666 378 \(1.8\times \)
Multi-record \(N{=}2\) 280 1204 498 \(2.4\times \)
Multi-record \(N{=}4\) 560 2266 617 \(3.7\times \)
Multi-record \(N{=}8\) 1120 4390 856 \(5.1\times \)

The \(5.4\times \) advantage at \(L{=}1024\) in the paper is within the range predicted by the cost laws for any task producing that output length, forced or natural. What the forcing artifact changes is not the ratio but the interpretation: at forced \(L{=}1024\) the AR arm is charged for discarded tokens; at natural \(L{=}1024\) (multi-record \(N{=}8\)) it is charged for tokens that are all used. The niche is real either way.

Caveat. This is an analytical projection, not a measurement. The cost laws are fitted on single-record output; multi-record output may have different prompt structure (longer input) and different token efficiency per record. The projection assumes the per-token decode cost is unchanged, which is the standard assumption for autoregressive decoding.

I Why the batching ceiling is likely architectural

The diffusion batching ceiling (\(\leq 1.7\times \)) is measured on two engine-bounded stacks (Appendix C). We argue here that the ceiling is likely architectural, not an engine bug, by reasoning about the mechanism rather than measuring it.

The KV-cache reuse argument. Autoregressive batching is cheap because of KV-cache reuse: when two requests share a prompt prefix, the prefill is computed once and the KV cache is shared. Even without prefix sharing, each request’s KV cache persists across decode steps, so the GPU alternates between requests at low marginal cost per token. Block-diffusion invalidates both forms of reuse:

1.
Cross-request prefix sharing requires that both requests are at the same denoising step. vLLM’s mixed-phase crash is an engine bug, but even if it were fixed, requests at different \(K\) steps cannot share a KV cache because the attention mask differs per step.
2.
Within-request KV persistence across denoising steps requires that the keys and values for prompt tokens are identical across steps. They are — prompt tokens are never masked — so in principle the prompt KV could be cached. But the denoising tokens’ KV changes at every step (the mask changes), so the per-step computation cannot reuse the previous step’s activations for the denoising positions.

The net effect: each denoising step is a fresh forward pass over the full sequence (prompt + denoising positions), with a different attention mask. The GPU does the same FLOPs whether batching or not, because the attention pattern is request-specific and step-specific. This is structurally different from AR decoding, where the attention pattern is causal and the KV cache grows monotonically.

The cache-off test confirms the direction. Disabling prefix caching on the AR arm drops its multiplier from \(42.8\times \) to \(26.8\times \) — still \(20\times \) above the DG ceiling. Cache reuse accounts for part of the AR advantage but not all of it; the remainder is the bandwidth-bound decode loop, which DG also lacks because each step is a full forward pass.

What a fixed engine would change. If vLLM’s mixed-phase crash were fixed (phase-segregated batching), the DG multiplier might rise above \(1.7\times \) — but only to the extent that requests at the same \(K\) step can share prompt prefill. The per-step denoising computation remains request-specific. The ceiling would likely move from \(\sim 1\times \) to \(\sim 2\)–\(3\times \), still an order of magnitude below AR’s \(26\)–\(43\times \). The “true ceiling is unknown” framing in Appendix C is honest; this appendix adds the structural argument for why it is unlikely to be high.

Caveat. This is a structural argument, not a measurement. A patched vLLM or a third diffusion serving stack could produce a different number. The argument says the ceiling is unlikely to reach AR’s level, not that it cannot improve at all.
