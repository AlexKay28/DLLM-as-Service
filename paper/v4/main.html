Diffusion LLMs as Fast Models for Specific Tasks:
The Cost Shape of Block-Structured Decoding

Aleksandr Kaigorodov
Yandex
August 2026

Abstract

Block-diffusion language models decode entire blocks of tokens in parallel through iterative denoising, breaking the linear per-token latency scaling that governs autoregressive (AR) decoding. We show that this produces a fundamentally different cost shape: AR latency is a line in output length with no floor; block-diffusion latency is a staircase — flat inside a fixed-size block, stepping at each boundary. The shapes cross at 6 output tokens, above which diffusion is faster.

We measure both architectures on a pinned serving stack (vLLM, A100 80GB) for structured log extraction. Both arms are Mixture-of-Experts: DiffusionGemma-26B-A4B (4 B active of 26 B) and Qwen3-30B-A3B (3 B active of 30 B). Against this fair MoE-vs-MoE baseline, the diffusion model wins at every practical output length in serial mode: \(15.6\times \) faster at the natural output length of 123 tokens, \(8.0\times \) at \(L{=}1024\). It degrades under deadline pressure by losing quality rather than completeness — a property AR decoding has no equivalent for.

The advantage is limited by deployment factors: the AR model batches \(77\times \) better and has a \(3.21\times \) tighter tail. But in the regime where batching is unavailable (serial investigation chains, autonomous driving, algorithmic trading), the staircase shape provides a structural latency advantage that the line cannot match. We state five conditions that define this regime and ground them in concrete workloads.

1 Introduction

Autoregressive (AR) language models generate text one token at a time; each token requires a forward pass over the growing KV cache. Block-diffusion models generate an entire block of tokens in parallel through iterative denoising, then step to the next block. We call this second class block-diffusion because the block structure — not the diffusion process — is what determines its cost shape.

We compare the two on a pinned serving stack: vLLM, NVIDIA A100 80GB PCIe, BF16. The task is structured log extraction from the LOGHUB BGL corpus — a collection of system logs from the BlueGene/L supercomputer at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. Each raw log line describes a hardware or software event (e.g., “kernel: RAS-MODULE: data storage interrupt on node R21-M0-NF-C:J11-U11”). The model must extract a structured JSON record with 12 fields:

  • Structural (7): timestamp, host, component, severity, message template, error code, metric value

  • Semantic (2): event type, affected entity

  • Anomaly (2): is_anomaly (boolean), anomaly_score (float)

  • Action (1): remediation hint

The structural fields require span-copying from the input (the model must locate and transcribe substrings like hostnames and timestamps). The semantic fields require inference (classifying the event type from free-text descriptions). The anomaly fields require binary classification. Quality is measured as macro-averaged token-level F1 across 9 of the 12 fields; the other 3 (event_type, affected_entity, remediation_hint) have degenerate gold labels on BGL (2, 1, and 1 unique values across 2,000 events respectively), so a model that emits an empty object scores \(1.000\) on two of them by trivially matching a constant. All experiments use \(n = 300\) BGL test events per cell unless stated otherwise.

Both arms are Mixture-of-Experts models:

  • dg (DiffusionGemma-26B-A4B, 4.0 B active of 26 B total, 52 GB) — the block-diffusion arm. Top-8 of 128 experts.

  • ar-30b (Qwen3-30B-A3B, 3.0 B active of 30 B total, 60 GB) — the autoregressive arm. Matched to DG on architecture class (both MoE) and active parameters (3 B vs 4 B).

The cost shapes we derive are properties of the decoding paradigm (token-by-token vs block-by-block), not of model scale. A larger AR model would have a steeper slope but the same linear shape; a larger diffusion model would have a higher floor but the same staircase.

Our contributions are as follows:

  • How do the two architectures differ in latency cost? We derive two cost laws from measurement: AR latency is a line in output length with no floor; block-diffusion latency is a staircase, flat inside a fixed-size block and stepping at each boundary. The shapes cross at \(L^{*} = \crossoverL \) output tokens (§2).

  • Where is the diffusion model faster? Above \(L^{*} = \crossoverL \) tokens, DG is faster. The advantage remains large: \(15.6\times \) at natural output, \(8.0\times \) at \(L{=}1024\). The advantage holds at naturally-long output (multi-record extraction) without forcing (§3).

  • What limits the diffusion model’s advantage in practice? The AR model batches \(77\times \) better and has a \(3.21\times \) tighter tail. But in the batch-1 regime — serial chains, autonomous driving, trading — batching is unavailable and the staircase shape dominates (§4).

  • When is block diffusion the right choice? When output is long (\(L \geq 1000\)), batch-1 is required, and schema completeness matters. We state five conditions and ground them in concrete workloads (§5).

2 The two cost laws: a line and a staircase

2.1 Parallel formulation

Both architectures decode output tokens from the same prompt, but their costs accumulate differently. For autoregressive decoding, each token requires one forward pass over the growing KV cache. For block-diffusion decoding, each canvas of \(C\) tokens is denoised in parallel through \(K\) steps, then the model steps to the next canvas.

In general form, both cost laws share the structure: \begin {equation} \label {eq:general} T = a + b \cdot f(L_{\mathrm {out}}) \end {equation} where:

  • \(T\) is the per-event latency in milliseconds,

  • \(L_{\mathrm {out}}\) is the output length in tokens,

  • \(a\) is the fixed cost (intercept / floor) independent of output length,

  • \(b\) is the marginal cost per unit of output,

  • \(f(L_{\mathrm {out}})\) is the output-length function: linear for AR (\(f = L_{\mathrm {out}}\)), staircase for DG (\(f = \lceil L_{\mathrm {out}}/C \rceil - 1\)),

  • \(C\) is the canvas size (tokens per block, \(C = \canvasLength \) for DiffusionGemma),

  • \(K\) is the number of denoising steps per canvas (\(K = 8\) at the operating point).

The measured cost laws are: \begin {align} \label {eq:ar} T_{\textsc {ar}}(L_{\mathrm {out}}) &= \arBase + \arSlope \cdot L_{\mathrm {out}} && \text {(ms, linear)} \\ \label {eq:dg} T_{\textsc {dg}}(L_{\mathrm {out}}) &= \dgBase + \dgStep \cdot \Bigl (\bigl \lceil \tfrac {L_{\mathrm {out}}}{\canvasLength }\bigr \rceil - 1\Bigr ) && \text {(ms, staircase)} \end {align}

For AR: \(a = \arBase \) ms is the prompt prefill cost (amortised by prefix caching). The low value reflects the MoE architecture’s efficient prefill: only 3 B of 30 B parameters are active per token, so the prefill computation is much cheaper than for a dense model of comparable total size. \(b = \arSlope \) ms is the per-token decode cost — one KV cache forward pass per token. Every output token costs exactly \(b\), regardless of position.

For DG: \(a = \dgBase \) ms (first-canvas cost: prompt prefill paid \(K\) times, once per denoising step, plus \(K\) denoising passes over \(C\) tokens). \(b = \dgStep \) ms (marginal cost of one additional canvas — a fresh denoising pass over a new block of \(C\) tokens). Within a canvas, \(b = 0\): additional tokens are free because they are denoised in parallel. This is why \(a\) is much larger for DG than AR, while \(b\) is zero within a block.

The operating point \(K{=}8\) was selected as the latency/quality knee: quality is flat above \(K{=}8\) (0.853 at \(K{=}8\), 0.858 at \(K{=}32\)) while latency saturates above \(K{=}16\). Each additional canvas costs 119.5 ms (median of 7 measured transitions, range 113.1–122.6).

The key structural difference: within a canvas, DG emits tokens for free (parallel denoising), while AR pays \(\arSlope \) ms per token. At canvas boundaries, DG pays a fixed \(\dgStep \) ms step, while AR’s cost is unaffected. The shapes cross where the two costs are equal (\(T_{\textsc {ar}} = T_{\textsc {dg}}\)). Solving for \(L_{\mathrm {out}}\): \begin {equation} \label {eq:lcross} L^{*} = \frac {a_{\textsc {dg}} - a_{\textsc {ar}}}{b_{\textsc {ar}}} = \frac {\dgBase - \arBase }{\arSlope } = \crossoverL \text { tokens}, \qquad \lambda ^{*} = \frac {1000}{a_{\textsc {dg}}} = \frac {1000}{\dgBase } = \crossoverLambda \text { events/s} \end {equation} where \(L^{*}\) is the output length at which both architectures produce equal latency (the crossover point), \(a_{\textsc {ar}}\) and \(a_{\textsc {dg}}\) are the fixed costs (intercepts) from Eqs. ??–??, \(b_{\textsc {ar}}\) is the AR per-token slope, and \(\lambda ^{*}\) is the maximum serial event rate before a queue forms (the reciprocal of the DG floor \(a_{\textsc {dg}}\), in events per second). Below \(L^{*}\), AR is cheaper; above it, DG is — until the next canvas edge, where the gap widens again.

2.2 What the base is made of

The 378.8 ms base is 37–53% prefill (by linear interpolation between \(K{=}2\) and \(K{=}8\); see Appendix B). Each denoising step re-attends the full prompt, so prefill is paid \(K\) times. If the prompt KV were cached across denoising steps, the base could fall toward \(\sim \)150 ms and \(L^{*}\) would move downward. We treat the measured base as the operative number; see Appendix H for the asymmetry with the batching caveat.

2.3 Generalisation

Both laws generalise from BGL to HDFS within 1.7%. The AR generalisation was measured on the dense AR-3B (not AR-30B); the DG canvas step moves from 118.5 ms to 120.5 ms (\(+1.7\%\)).

3 Where the diffusion model wins

The diffusion model’s latency advantage is dramatic in serial (batch-1) mode. The AR-30B MoE model takes \(\sim \)5900 ms to produce a 123-token JSON record; DG completes the same task in 379 ms — a \(15.6\times \) advantage. The AR model stops at \(\sim \)123 tokens (EOS), so latency plateaus regardless of max_tokens.

DG wins at every practical output length. The serial cost law gives crossover \(L^{*} = \crossoverL \) tokens — DG is faster at any output above 6 tokens. At the natural output length of 123 tokens, DG is \(15.6\times \) faster. The advantage narrows at longer output (DG pays per canvas) but remains large: \(8.0\times \) at \(L{=}1024\).

The AR-30B serial slope is 47.7 ms/token — 48\(\times \) steeper than the batch-throughput slope (1.0 ms/token). This is because serial mode pays full prefill and decode overhead per request, while batch mode amortises across concurrent requests. The batch-throughput projection severely understates AR serial latency.

Energy. The diffusion model uses less energy because it is busy for less time. The energy ratio is: \begin {equation} R_{\mathrm {energy}} = \frac {E_{\mathrm {AR}}}{E_{\mathrm {DG}}} \end {equation} where \(E_{\mathrm {AR}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm {DG}}\) are the net energy per event (joules, idle-subtracted) for each arm. Measured values: \begin {align} \text {AR-30B MoE:} \quad & R_{\mathrm {energy}} = \frac {10.1}{4.2} = 2.4 \quad \text {(idle 92.7\,W)} \\ \text {Dense AR-1.5B:} \quad & R_{\mathrm {energy}} = \frac {5.1}{4.2} = 1.2 \quad \text {(idle 58.6\,W)} \end {align}

A ratio > 1 means AR uses more energy. The three runs used different idle baselines (AR-30B MoE 92.7 W, AR-1.5B 58.6 W, DG 52.6 W); the DG energy was not re-measured at the AR-30B idle point, so the AR-30B vs DG ratio (2.4) is indicative rather than directly controlled.

Graceful degradation under deadline pressure. Under a tightened deadline the diffusion model returns a complete canvas at lower quality, while the AR model returns a truncated record that fails schema validation. At a p99 budget of 400 ms, dropping \(K\) from 8 to 4 meets it at 394.3 ms p99 with F1 falling from 0.853 to 0.790. Table 3 summarises the contrast across practical challenges.

4 What limits the advantage in practice

The diffusion model’s serial advantage is large (\(8.0\times \) at \(L{=}1024\)). The deployment factors that limit it are as follows:

1. Footprint. Both models are MoE and have comparable footprints: AR-30B at 60 GB vs DG at 52 GB. Neither can share a GPU with another large model. The asymmetry that matters is against the quality-similar dense AR-1.5B (3.0 GB): 16 copies fit in the space either MoE model occupies.

2. Batching. Continuous batching converts arrival concurrency into throughput at \(77\times \) for AR-30B against \(\leq 1.7\times \) for DG. Both DG measurements are engine-bounded (vLLM caps the batch at 8; dInfer runs cache-less), so the true ceiling is unknown but lies at or above \(1.7\times \). Even so, the gap is more than an order of magnitude (Appendix C).


Figure 3: Batch-1 to peak throughput multipliers (BGL, \(n{=}300\)). AR-30B MoE reaches \(77\times \); DG caps at \(\leq 1.7\times \). The prefix cache on/off bars are the dense AR-3B (the only arm the cache test was run on), not the AR-30B MoE.

3. Tail latency. The diffusion arm’s per-event coefficient of variation is 20.9% with a max/median ratio of 3.21\(\times \), against the AR arm’s 0.2% and 1.03\(\times \) (Appendix G). A \(3.21\times \) tail is what breaks a batch-1 SLA — exactly the regime where DG’s per-event advantage applies.

4. Input length. The diffusion prefill slope is 2.6\(\times \) steeper than AR’s, because each denoising step re-attends the full prompt. At longer inputs the base rises and \(L^{*}\) moves further upward.

5. Co-residency. If batch-1 is imposed by tenant isolation, the relevant alternative is not the MoE AR-30B but sixteen copies of the quality-similar dense AR-1.5B (3 GB each). Per-tenant latency is unchanged; throughput is \(16\times \); isolation is preserved.

5 What survives

The MoE-matched comparison shows DG’s advantage is concentrated at serial mode. DG wins at every practical output length: \(15.6\times \) at natural output, \(8.0\times \) at \(L{=}1024\). The five conditions that define the regime where block diffusion is the right choice are:

1.
Output length \(\geq 6\) tokens (DG crossover in serial mode).
2.
Batch-1 by requirement (tenant isolation, interactivity, or serial chain structure).
3.
Completeness is validated against a schema.
4.
The quality floor exceeds what the AR model delivers.
5.
A 52.0 GB resident footprint is acceptable.

Concrete workloads. Serial investigation chains. A security operations centre receives an alert, looks up threat intelligence, cross-references internal logs, and drills into the next layer. Each hop blocks on the previous; concurrency is 1 by construction. DG’s per-hop p50 is \(\sim \)410 ms (Figure 4, recorded against the dense AR-3B baseline, not AR-30B). Against AR-30B the per-hop cost projected from the serial cost law (Eq. ??) is \(\sim \)5946 ms at natural record length, so DG is \(\sim \)14\(\times \) faster per hop. The advantage compounds with chain depth: at \(D \geq 8\) the cumulative output exceeds 1000 tokens, where DG’s staircase cost grows far more slowly than AR’s linear per-token cost.


Figure 4: Serial investigation chains (BGL, \(n{=}300\), \(K{=}8\); AR arm is the dense AR-3B baseline). A: per-hop p50 is constant across depth. B: DG’s p99.9/p50 ratio converges to AR’s mean as depth increases.

Critical-alert preemption. A FATAL log line arrives mid-stream. DG’s bounded service time (\(\sim \)379 ms at \(L{=}256\), \(K{=}8\)) and complete-canvas guarantee make it suitable for priority preemption: a preempted request returns a valid record, not a truncated one.

Autonomous driving. One vehicle, one scene, one decision: batch-1 by construction. Output is long (multi-object scene description, predicted trajectories). The \(K\) knob is a per-request quality slider with two usable positions (\(K{=}8\) and \(K{=}4\)): drop \(K\) to meet a tightened deadline without truncating output. The completeness guarantee holds only above the canvas length; below \(L{=}128\) DG’s schema validity collapses (1.0%, see Appendix F).

Algorithmic trading. A serial chain with a 1–5 s market reaction window. At 8+ hops (\(L \geq 1000\)), DG’s staircase advantage provides faster end-to-end analysis.

Wide-schema normalisation. DG’s joint-canvas denoising treats all fields as one structured object. We measured schema widths \(F \in \{12, 25, 50, 100\}\): DG’s latency advantage was at \(1.8\)–\(3.0\times \) across widths (measured against the dense AR-3B baseline, not AR-30B).


Figure 5: Latency vs schema width (BGL, \(n{=}300\)).

Where diffusion does not win. High-rate log streams (\(\lambda \gg 2.6\) ev/s) favour AR’s batching by \(77\times \). Tail-sensitive single-stream workloads with strict p99 SLAs are hurt by DG’s \(3.21\times \) max/median ratio. And any workload that can batch favours AR regardless of output length.

Where diffusion fails. Six hypotheses about diffusion’s structural advantages were tested and refuted (Appendix D). The most instructive failures:

  • Input perturbation robustness: bidirectional attention propagates input noise in both directions. DG degraded more than AR in all 10 perturbation categories, by up to \(8.2\times \) the AR drop.

  • Infilling: DG’s joint denoising should preserve suffix tokens by construction. In practice, AR trivially regenerates the whole record from the prompt (F1 0.99–1.0).

  • Iterative refinement: selective re-masking gains \(+0.023\) F1 over three rounds, but after three rounds DG still trails AR after one pass. The mechanism is real; the win is not.

These failures define the operational boundary: block diffusion’s advantage is in latency shape at long output, not in robustness, infilling, or iterative refinement.

6 Related work

Diffusion LM speedup claims (LLaDA [7], DiffusionGemma [3]) are typically measured against author-built AR baselines. Our contribution is to pin both arms on the same serving stack and derive cost laws from measurement rather than from single-point comparisons.

Constrained decoding (outlines [8], xgrammar [2], llguidance [6]) is the standard answer to the structured-output problem and is not measured here; it is a stated limitation (§7). Speculative decoding (Medusa [1], EAGLE [5]) attacks the same per-token slope the AR cost law turns on; it changes the slope, not the shape.

[4] surveys reproducibility pitfalls in systems measurement. Our methodological contribution (Appendix A) — eight priced corrections found by re-reading committed artifacts — is a case study in that tradition, not a general method.

7 Threats to validity

All measurements are on one GPU (NVIDIA A100 80GB PCIe); the fitted constants are A100-specific but the shapes should transfer. The crossover is calibrated at a \(\sim \)1,047-token prompt and shifts with input length. The AR–DG quality difference (\(\Delta = \qualityGap \), 95% CI \([-0.012, +0.011]\), \(n = 300\)) is equivalent at a \(\pm 0.015\) margin. This quality comparison uses the dense AR-3B baseline (Qwen2.5-3B), not the AR-30B MoE: the 9-field F1 of 0.852 was re-scored with the corrected first-object-wins parser on the dense baseline, and the AR-30B MoE was not re-scored at the same parser setting. The latency comparison (Table 2) uses the AR-30B MoE cost law, so the quality and latency arms are not the same model.

The surviving niche has not been tested on a task whose natural output length is \(\geq 512\) tokens. The AR-30B cost law (Eq. ??) is fitted from a five-point serial (batch-1) sweep with \(R^2 = 1.0\), but the model stops at \(\sim \)123 tokens (EOS), so the slope is carried by the \(L{=}32\) point and the cluster at natural output. The measured serial p50 at \(L{=}256\) is 5899 ms, matching the cost law evaluated at the actual output of 123 tokens (5946 ms). The advantage in serial mode (15.6\(\times \) at natural output) is the realized comparison; forcing AR to generate longer output would increase its latency per the cost law, making DG’s advantage larger. A task with genuinely long natural output — multi-record extraction, incident reports — would test the niche without this EOS-stop artifact (Appendix H provides an analytical projection).

The niche has not been tested against grammar-constrained AR decoding (outlines, xgrammar, llguidance), which would eliminate over-generation, guarantee 100% schema validity, and remove the diffusion model’s completeness advantage. An attempt to run grammar-constrained ar-1.5b on vLLM 0.10.2 produced 1.0% schema-valid output — the grammar enforced JSON structure but the model padded string fields it could not answer, preventing the JSON from closing. This is a stated limitation.

The batching ceiling reproduces across two runtimes and two model families, but both measurements are engine-bounded (Appendix C). A structural argument for why the ceiling is likely architectural — not an engine bug — is in Appendix I.

The cost-shape generalisation (“any decoder emitting fixed-size blocks would inherit the same shape”) is inferred from one block-diffusion model measured against one AR family. The shape claim is structural but not tested on a second diffusion family.

8 Conclusion

Block-diffusion language models have a staircase cost shape that is structurally different from the linear cost of autoregressive decoding. The shapes cross at \(L^{*} = \crossoverL \) output tokens, above which diffusion is faster. The advantage is large: \(15.6\times \) at natural output, \(8.0\times \) at \(L{=}1024\).

Against a MoE-matched AR model (Qwen3-30B-A3B, 3 B active of 30 B), this is a fair comparison. Moving from the dense AR-3B to the MoE AR-30B raised the serial per-token cost from 6.3 to 47.7 ms/token (\(7.6\times \)), pushing the crossover from 59 to 6 tokens. DG wins at every practical output length in serial mode.

The advantage is limited where batching is available (AR batches \(77\times \) better) and where tail SLAs are tight (DG’s max/median is \(3.21\times \)). But in the batch-1 regime — serial investigation chains, autonomous driving, algorithmic trading — the staircase shape provides a structural latency advantage that the line cannot match. The \(K\) knob gives DG a per-request quality slider that AR has no equivalent for, and the complete-canvas guarantee under deadline pressure is a qualitative property.

Block diffusion is the right choice when output is long, batch-1 is required, and schema completeness matters. We have named five conditions and grounded them in concrete workloads.

A Eight priced corrections

Each of the following was found by re-reading committed artifacts, not by running new experiments. Items 1–5 were harness defects — the measurement itself was contaminated. Items 6–8 were claims withdrawn after correction — the measurement was sound, but the claim drawn from it was not. Five of the eight biased against the autoregressive arm.

1.
Decoding configuration. Qwen’s checkpoint sets repetition_penalty\(=\) 1.1, which suppresses span-copying and empties the host field. Correcting to 1.0 moved AR 9-field F1 from 0.779 to 0.844. Against AR.
2.
Serving parity. The AR model ran a naive HuggingFace generate loop while the diffusion model ran on vLLM with continuous batching. Moving AR to vLLM moved its p50 from 1841.6 to 413.3 ms (4.5\(\times \)), reversing the headline speed claim. Against AR.
3.
Parser policy. At \(L{=}512\), 96% of AR outputs contained multiple JSON objects. The old parser took the last (most-drifted) copy; first-object-wins moved AR F1 from 0.599 to 0.826. Against AR.
4.
Metric defaults. The completeness metric treated falsy values ("", 0.0, False) as missing keys. The AR model emits falsy defaults on early stop; DiffusionGemma always fills every field. Distinguishing absent from present-but-falsy moved AR completeness from 0% to 100%. Against AR.
5.
Provenance. A CLI default wrote an AR model name into three DG result files. Biases no comparison but made audit impossible until correction sidecars were written. Neutral.
6.
Classical-parser baseline. An earlier draft claimed a classical parser matched LLM extraction at Field-F1 of \(0.68\) against \(0.85\). What was recorded as a near-match was self-grading: our “Drain” was a regex fallback rather than the reference implementation, and the gold generator was the same code. The claim is withdrawn. Neutral (inflated a third baseline, not either architecture). Found by re-reading the gold-generation script.
7.
Contaminated semantic gold. An earlier draft claimed structural and semantic fields separate cleanly, on a 12-field macro where AR led DG by 0.041 F1. The semantic gold labels were placeholder output of the same parser, one a constant across 2,000 events. On the decontaminated 9-field macro the two differ by 0.000. The contamination inflated the arm that fills fields with plausible text (AR) and penalised the arm that leaves them empty (DG). Against DG. Found by re-reading the gold labels.
8.
Queueing model. An earlier draft claimed an M/D/1 queue predicts diffusion latency under load. The match was to the measured saturation knee under Poisson arrivals; under bursty (MMPP) arrivals, which is what log streams actually produce, the model stops being predictive. The claim is withdrawn. Against AR (overstated how well DG’s load behaviour is modelled).

The clustering on one arm is not a coincidence: a comparison harness written by someone hoping the new architecture wins fails asymmetrically, because the arm the experimenter is not rooting for inherits the checkpoint defaults, the naive serving path, and the mature production infrastructure.

B Base decomposition: why the fit fails

Sweeping \(K \in \{2,4,8,16,32,64\}\) at fixed output length fits \(T(K) = \kfitBase + \kfitSlope \cdot K\) with \(R^2 = \kfitRsq \). The low \(R^2\) is itself informative: latency saturates above \(K{=}16\), so the linear term is a poor description at the top of the range and the fit is carried by the bottom. The intercept is therefore not interpretable as a floor.


Figure 7: Denoising-step sweep at \(L_{\mathrm {out}}{=}\canvasLength \) (BGL, \(n{=}200\) per point). Latency saturates above \(K{=}16\); quality is flat above \(K{=}8\). Halving \(K\) from 8 to 4 saves \(\sim \)87 ms because the saving comes out of the small slice, while F1 falls from 0.853 to 0.790 because the loss comes out of the whole.

Two measurement-based bounds do the work instead:

  • From \(K{=}2\). For any decomposition \(T(K) = \text {floor} + K \cdot c(K)\) with \(c \geq 0\), \(\text {floor} \leq T(2) = 202\) ms. At \(K{=}8\) the denoising-attributable share is \(\geq 47\%\) of the 378.8 ms base.

  • From the canvas step. One additional canvas costs 119.5 ms and is a \(K{=}8\) denoising pass. So denoising is \(\geq \dgStep / \dgBase = 32\%\) of the base. The first canvas’s pass is somewhat cheaper than a marginal one, but not by the \(5\times \) the fitted intercept would require.

The conservative bounds at \(K{=}8\) are denoising \(\geq 32\%\) (canvas step) and \(\geq 47\%\) (\(K{=}2\)), so the floor is at most \(68\%\) and \(53\%\) respectively. A tighter estimate comes from linear interpolation between \(K{=}2\) and \(K{=}8\): solving \(T(2) = \text {prefill} + 2 \cdot \text {step} = 201.7\) and \(T(8) = \text {prefill} + 8 \cdot \text {step} = 378.6\) gives step \(= 29.5\) ms and prefill \(= 142.7\) ms, i.e. prefill \(= 38\%\) and denoising \(= 62\%\) . The exact split awaiting profiler attribution is now narrowed to prefill 37–53%, denoising 47–63% — far from the old 93.5%/6.5% claim the fitted intercept produced.

C The batching ceiling and its engine defects

The diffusion batching ceiling (\(\leq \)1.7\(\times \)) reproduces across two runtimes and two model families, but both measurements are bounded by known engine defects:

  • vLLM caps the batch at 8 when two requests mix denoising phases, so the DiffusionGemma multipliers (1.5–1.7\(\times \)) are lower bounds, not ceiling measurements.

  • dInfer (LLaDA-8B) errors on prompts of 632 tokens or more, forcing a cache-less mode that inflates absolute latency by \(\sim \)30\(\times \). The multiplier (1.2\(\times \)) is unaffected as a ratio, but the claim that this is a cache-enabled measurement is not defensible.

The standing explanation — that bidirectional attention invalidates KV-cache reuse — is partly falsified: disabling prefix caching on the AR arm drops its multiplier from 42.8\(\times \) to 26.8\(\times \), still more than twenty times the diffusion ceiling. Remaining candidates include mixture-of-experts routing variability and kernel maturity. The true ceiling on an uncrippled stack is unknown but lies at or above 1.7\(\times \).

D Six refuted hypotheses

We set out to find regimes where the diffusion model beats the autoregressive baseline, and generated hypotheses accordingly. Six were refuted. They are reported here at the same length as the confirmations, because a paper whose central methodological claim is “price the baseline before you compare against it” has exactly one asset, and that asset is spent the moment a negative result gets softened into a footnote. The distinction between these six and the eight-item ledger in Appendix A is worth stating plainly. There, our harness was wrong: the measurement itself was contaminated, so the recorded number was incorrect. Here, our prediction was wrong: the measurement was sound, but the hypothesis it tested did not survive it.

The measurement discipline is not a preamble to the results. It is what separates these six from the ones we kept.

E The input-length variable and the canvas Pareto

E.1 Input length: the second variable

Equation ?? has two variables, and the second one matters more for diffusion than for autoregression. Sweeping the prompt from roughly 1,000 to 6,800 tokens gives 0.0 ms per prompt token for the autoregressive arm and 0.1 ms for the diffusion arm — 2.6\(\times \) steeper. The mechanism is direct: the autoregressive model pays for the prompt once and every generated token then attends to a cached KV, whereas each denoising step re-attends the full prompt, so prefill is paid \(K\) times.


Figure 8: Input-length sweep at fixed \(L_{\mathrm {out}}{=}\canvasLength \) (BGL, \(n{=}100\) per point; AR arm is the dense AR-3B baseline). The prompt is lengthened by repeating the schema block, so the task is unchanged. B is the finding: the diffusion model’s prefill slope is 2.6\(\times \) the autoregressive model’s.

Every one-variable statement in this paper — the crossover \(L^{*}\), the per-event ratios — is calibrated at the reference prompt of \(\sim \)1,047 tokens. At longer inputs the diffusion base rises 2.6\(\times \) faster and pushes \(L^{*}\) upward. Extrapolated to a fixed \(L_{\mathrm {out}}{=}\canvasLength \), the two curves meet near \(L_{\mathrm {in}} \approx 18{,}000\) tokens, beyond which the autoregressive model is cheaper at any output length. We did not measure that far; the number is an extrapolation of two fitted slopes and is quoted as an order of magnitude, not a boundary.

E.2 A free Pareto improvement at canvas \(= 128\)

DiffusionGemma’s canvas length is a configuration parameter, so we swept it. At canvas \(=128\) the base falls to 385.5 ms (\(-2.0\%\) relative to the canvas \(=256\) measurement of 378.8 ms; the canvas sweep’s own \(L{=}256\) point measured 376.0 ms in a separate run, \(n{=}200\)) and 9-field F1 rises to 0.864 — an improvement on both axes simultaneously. At canvas \(=64\) the base rises \(48\%\), because the per-canvas fixed overhead is paid twice as often; that setting is not viable. This is the only direct experimental evidence that block size, not denoising schedule, is the interesting design variable. Neither canvas setting moves the per-event crossover. \(L^{*}\) is set by the prefill cost that dominates the base, and prefill does not shrink when the canvas does. Tuning the canvas trades quality against step count; it does not buy a different crossover.

G Latency distributions

The per-event latency distributions are asymmetric. Across the 300 individual events of one anchor run at \(L{=}\canvasLength \):

  • AR-30B per-event CV: 0.2%; max/median: 1.03\(\times \).

  • DG-26B-A4B per-event CV: 20.9%; max/median: 3.21\(\times \).

The diffusion arm’s 3.21\(\times \) max/median ratio is driven by mixture-of-experts routing variability and warm-up on the first events of a run. Both models are deterministic at temperature 0 — across five repeats of 300 events, outputs were identical — so the tail is a property of the serving path, not of the model’s stochasticity. The AR arm’s 0.2% CV means its p50 is a reliable planning number; the DG arm’s 20.9% means a p99 SLA lands in a very different place than p50 would suggest (BGL, \(n{=}300\)).

H Analytical resolution of the forced-decoding question

The comparison in Table 2 uses forced lengths for AR-30B for min_tokens output that the parser discards. A reviewer asks: does the advantage survive on a task whose natural output length is \(\geq 512\) tokens? We answer this analytically, without running a new experiment, by applying the fitted cost laws.

The cost laws are length-dependent, not forcing-dependent. AR-30B’s latency is linear in output length, fitted from a five-point serial (batch-1) output-length sweep (\(R^2 = 1.0\); the model generates \({\sim }123\) tokens naturally, so four of the five points cluster at the plateau and the slope is carried by the \(L{=}32\) point): \begin {equation} T_{\textsc {ar}}(L) = a_{\textsc {ar}} + b_{\textsc {ar}} \cdot L = 79.1 + 47.7 \cdot L \quad \text {(ms)} \end {equation} where \(T_{\textsc {ar}}\) is the p50 latency, \(L\) is the output length in tokens, \(a_{\textsc {ar}} = 79.1\) ms is the prompt prefill cost, and \(b_{\textsc {ar}} = 47.7\) ms/token is the per-token serial decode cost of AR-30B MoE. DG’s latency is the staircase: \begin {equation} T_{\textsc {dg}}(L) = a_{\textsc {dg}} + b_{\textsc {dg}} \cdot \Bigl (\bigl \lceil \tfrac {L}{C} \bigr \rceil - 1\Bigr ) = \dgBase + \dgStep \cdot \Bigl (\bigl \lceil \tfrac {L}{\canvasLength } \bigr \rceil - 1\Bigr ) \quad \text {(ms)} \end {equation} where \(a_{\textsc {dg}} = \dgBase \) ms is the first-canvas cost (prefill + \(K{=}8\) denoising steps over \(C = \canvasLength \) tokens), \(b_{\textsc {dg}} = \dgStep \) ms is the marginal cost per additional canvas, and \(\lceil L/C \rceil \) counts the number of canvases needed. Neither law depends on min_tokens; both depend only on \(L\).

A naturally-long task exists. A multi-record extraction task — emit a JSON array of \(N\) structured records from \(N\) consecutive log lines — produces output of \(N \times 140\) tokens naturally, without forcing. At \(N{=}4\) the output is \(\sim \)560 tokens; at \(N{=}8\), \(\sim \)1120.

The advantage holds. Applying the fitted laws:

Task \(L_{\mathrm {out}}\) AR-30B (ms) DG (ms) Ratio
Single record (unforced) 123 5900 379 DG \(15.6\times \)
Multi-record \(N{=}2\) 280 13435 498 DG \(27.0\times \)
Multi-record \(N{=}4\) 560 26791 618 DG \(43.4\times \)
Multi-record \(N{=}8\) 1120 53503 857 DG \(62.4\times \)

With the MoE-matched AR-30B in serial mode, DG wins at every output length. The advantage is \(15.6\times \) at natural output and grows to \(62.4\times \) at \(N{=}8\), because AR’s linear cost (\(47.7\) ms/token) grows far faster than DG’s staircase (\(119.5\) ms per \(256\) tokens). The first row is the measured baseline; subsequent rows are cost-law projections at the multi-record output lengths.

Caveat. These are analytical projections from the fitted cost laws, not direct measurements of multi-record extraction. The laws are fitted on single-record output; multi-record output may have different prompt structure.

I Why the batching ceiling is likely architectural

The diffusion batching ceiling (\(\leq 1.7\times \)) is measured on two engine-bounded stacks (Appendix C). We argue here that the ceiling is likely architectural, not an engine bug, by reasoning about the mechanism rather than measuring it.

The KV-cache reuse argument. Autoregressive batching is cheap because of KV-cache reuse: when two requests share a prompt prefix, the prefill is computed once and the KV cache is shared. Even without prefix sharing, each request’s KV cache persists across decode steps, so the GPU alternates between requests at low marginal cost per token. Block-diffusion invalidates both forms of reuse:

1.
Cross-request prefix sharing requires that both requests are at the same denoising step. vLLM’s mixed-phase crash is an engine bug, but even if it were fixed, requests at different \(K\) steps cannot share a KV cache because the attention mask differs per step.
2.
Within-request KV persistence across denoising steps requires that the keys and values for prompt tokens are identical across steps. They are — prompt tokens are never masked — so in principle the prompt KV could be cached. But the denoising tokens’ KV changes at every step (the mask changes), so the per-step computation cannot reuse the previous step’s activations for the denoising positions.

The net effect: each denoising step is a fresh forward pass over the full sequence (prompt + denoising positions), with a different attention mask. The GPU does the same FLOPs whether batching or not, because the attention pattern is request-specific and step-specific. This is structurally different from AR decoding, where the attention pattern is causal and the KV cache grows monotonically.

The cache-off test confirms the direction. Disabling prefix caching on the AR arm drops its multiplier from \(\cacheOnMult \times \) to \(\cacheOffMult \times \) — still more than twenty times the diffusion ceiling. Cache reuse accounts for part of the AR advantage but not all of it; the remainder is the bandwidth-bound decode loop, which DG also lacks because each step is a full forward pass.

What a fixed engine would change. If vLLM’s mixed-phase crash were fixed (phase-segregated batching), the DG multiplier might rise above \(1.7\times \) — but only to the extent that requests at the same \(K\) step can share prompt prefill. The per-step denoising computation remains request-specific. The ceiling would likely move from \(\sim 1\times \) to \(\sim 2\)–\(3\times \), still an order of magnitude below AR’s \(26\)–\(43\times \). The “true ceiling is unknown” framing in Appendix C is honest; this appendix adds the structural argument for why it is unlikely to be high.

Caveat. This is a structural argument, not a measurement. A patched vLLM or a third diffusion serving stack could produce a different number. The argument says the ceiling is unlikely to reach AR’s level, not that it cannot improve at all.
