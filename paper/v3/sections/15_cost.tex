\section{Cost per Event}
\label{sec:cost} The surviving niche requires batch-1 (condition 2) and a 52\,GB resident
footprint (condition 5) simultaneously --- an 80\,GB A100 dedicated to serving
one request at a time. Table~\ref{tab:cost} prices this at a published
on-demand rate. \begin{table}[h]
\centering

\label{tab:cost}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule

\midrule

\end{table} At 100\% duty cycle on a dedicated GPU, DG is $1.8\times$ cheaper per event
than AR-1.5B --- the same ratio as its latency advantage. But this comparison
ignores the footprint asymmetry that conditions 2 and 5 create. The AR model
occupies 3.0\,GB; 26 copies fit in the same 80\,GB device. At 16-way
co-residency (the natural response to tenant-isolated batch-1), the
effective cost per event drops to \$0.0001 --- $5\times$ cheaper than a
dedicated DG instance. The diffusion model cannot co-reside: at 52\,GB it
consumes the entire GPU. The break-even question is: at what duty cycle does DG's per-event advantage
pay for the GPU it monopolises? The answer is never, under the co-residency
alternative. A single DG instance at 100\% duty produces 9{,}504 events/hr at
\$0.0005/event. Sixteen AR-1.5B instances on the same GPU produce
$16 \times 5{,}408 = 86{,}528$ events/hr at \$0.0001/event. The AR
co-resident deployment is $9\times$ cheaper per event while preserving
tenant isolation. This does not eliminate the niche --- condition 2 says ``batch-1 by
requirement'', and if the requirement is architectural (one model instance,
not one per tenant) then co-residency is ruled out. But it narrows it
further: the surviving deployment must justify dedicating an 80\,GB GPU to a
single request stream where a 3.0\,GB alternative exists.
